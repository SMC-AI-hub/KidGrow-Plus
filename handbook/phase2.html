Chapter 3 — Breastfeeding Technique: Position, Latch & Troubleshooting

Most breastfeeding problems are technique problems, not supply problems. A good position + deep latch prevents sore nipples, drains the breast well (protecting supply), and keeps the baby satisfied. This chapter teaches the skills; Chapter 5 teaches how to check they are working.

3.1 Four principles of good positioning (for every hold)

  1. Baby's body in a line — ear, shoulder and hip straight, not twisted. The baby should not have to turn the head to reach the breast.
  2. Tummy to mummy — baby's chest against mother's chest/body, pulled in close ("hug the baby in").
  3. Nose to nipple at the start — line the nipple up with the baby's nose (not mouth) so the head tilts back slightly and the chin presses into the breast.
  4. Bring baby to breast, not breast to baby — mother sits/stands comfortably with back supported; use pillows or a folded cloth under the baby, not under the mother's shoulders. Support the baby's neck and shoulders — never push the back of the head.

3.2 Five positions — pick what fits the pair

Fig 3.1 — Five breastfeeding positions. There is no single "correct" hold; the right one keeps the baby's body aligned, chin pressed in, and the mother pain-free. Health workers: demonstrate at least two holds before discharge.

3.3 The latch: wide gape, deep and asymmetric

A good latch takes a big mouthful of breast, not just the nipple. Touch the nipple to the baby's upper lip, wait for a wide-open mouth (like a yawn), then hug the baby in quickly so the lower jaw lands well below the nipple. This "asymmetric" latch — more breast tissue below the nipple than above — is what protects nipples and drains milk.

Fig 3.2 — Deep vs shallow latch (cross-section schematic). Pain that lasts beyond the first minute, a pinched "lipstick-shaped" nipple after feeds, or clicking sounds all signal shallow latch: break suction with a clean finger and re-latch.

3.4 How often, how long?

  • 8–12 feeds per 24 hours in the first weeks — including at night. Night feeds raise prolactin and protect supply.
  • Watch the baby, not the clock. Let the baby finish the first breast (slows, releases, sleepy) before offering the second. Feed length varies (10–40 min); some babies take one breast, some two.
  • Cluster feeding (many short feeds, often evenings) and growth-spurt days (~2–3 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months) are normal — more frequent feeding builds more milk. They are not proof of "not enough milk".
  • Wake a sleepy newborn every 2–3 hours until birth weight is regained and output is good (see §5.5).

3.5 Hunger and fullness cues — feed early, stop at contentment

Fig 3.3 — Hunger-cue ladder. Fullness cues mirror it: hands relax and open, body softens, baby releases the breast and looks sleepy/content. A crying baby latches poorly — calm first.

3.6 Troubleshooting: the four common problems

Fig 3.4 — Troubleshooting visual guide. Rule of thumb: no fever + mother well → keep draining, observe 24–48 h; fever + ill → same-day care. Never advise abrupt weaning for mastitis or plugged ducts.

3.7 Nursing strikes (sudden breast refusal)

  • Common triggers: cold/blocked nose, ear infection or teething pain, thrush in baby's mouth, fast or slow milk flow, new soap/perfume, recent vaccination fussiness, menstruation (taste change), stress or long separation.
  • Manage: skin-to-skin, offer when sleepy/calm, try different holds and rooms, express to protect supply, rule out illness (ear tugging, fever, mouth sores → doctor). Most strikes pass in 2–4 days — do not force the baby onto the breast.

3.8 Special pairs: twins & post-caesarean

👶👶 Twins

  • Feed separately first until each latches well, then try tandem (double football).
  • Alternate breasts between babies across feeds for balanced stimulation.
  • Accept help; rest when babies sleep — supply follows demand, even for two.

🏥 After caesarean

  • Start as soon as mother is alert — skin-to-skin in recovery if possible.
  • Use side-lying or football to keep weight off the scar; a pillow shields the wound.
  • Most post-CS painkillers are breastfeeding-compatible — ask the doctor, don't skip feeds from fear.
🚫 What NOT to do in the early weeks
  • No rigid 4-hourly schedules — feed on cue, 8–12 times/day.
  • No water, honey, ghutti, gripe water, or animal milk as prelacteals — give only breast milk (medicines/vitamins as prescribed excepted).
  • No bottles, pacifiers, or nipple shields without trained guidance — especially before latch is established.
  • Don't stop breastfeeding for a maternal cold, fever, or most infections — continue with hand hygiene and masking; specific exceptions (see Ch.13) are decided by the doctor, not the family.
✅ Key messages — Chapter 3
  1. Tummy-to-mummy, nose-to-nipple, baby brought to breast — in every hold.
  2. Wide gape + asymmetric deep latch prevents most nipple pain and supply problems.
  3. Feed 8–12 times/24 h on early cues, day and night.
  4. No fever + mother well → keep draining and observe; fever + ill → same-day care.
  5. Never advise sudden weaning for engorgement, plugged ducts, or mastitis.
👩‍⚕️ For the health workerObserve a full feed before discharge: position (4 checks), latch (mouth, lips, chin, areola), suck–swallow pattern, and mother's comfort. Document problems found and the fix taught. Re-check at the day 3–5 visit — that is when engorgement and weight-loss questions peak.

Chapter 4 — Expressed Breast Milk: Pumping, Storing & Feeding

Expressed breast milk (EBM) lets a baby get mother's milk when direct feeding isn't possible — working mothers, sick or preterm babies, engorgement relief, and building supply. Done cleanly and stored correctly, EBM is safe and keeps most of milk's benefits. (Preterm-baby specifics: see Ch.6.)

4.1 When expression helps

  • Separation: mother's work/travel/school, baby in hospital.
  • Baby factors: preterm, sleepy, weak suck, cleft palate (with guidance), nursing strike.
  • Mother factors: engorgement relief, maintaining supply during illness, donating to a human milk bank.

4.2 Hand expression — every mother should learn this

  1. Wash hands with soap. Sit comfortably; have a clean wide-mouthed container ready.
  2. Help let-down: think of the baby (or look at a photo), gently massage the breast, apply a warm cloth for a minute.
  3. C-hold: thumb and fingers in a "C" about 2–3 cm back from the nipple (not on it).
  4. Press back → roll forward: push gently toward the chest wall, then roll thumb and fingers forward together. Release, repeat rhythmically. Rotate the C around the clock.
  5. Switch breasts every few minutes or when flow slows; total ~20–30 min for a full expression.
  6. Colostrum comes in drops — collect with a clean spoon or small cup; every drop counts.

⚠️ Flange (funnel) fit mattersThe nipple should move freely in the tunnel with little areola pulled in. Too-small flanges cause pain, white/blistered nipples, and poor output. Most brands sell multiple sizes — measure, don't guess.

4.4 Clean collection & containers

  • Wash hands before every expression; breasts need no special washing (daily bath is enough).
  • Use food-grade, BPA-free containers or breast-milk storage bags; wash with soap + rinse, air-dry. Sterilize parts daily for babies under 3 months, preterm, or ill.
  • Label every container: date, time, volume. Leave ~2 cm headspace (milk expands when frozen).
  • Cool fresh milk before adding it to already-chilled milk; store in small portions (60–120 mL) to avoid waste.

4.7 Feeding the EBM: cup first, paced bottle if needed

🥛 Cup / paladai (preferred early)

  • Standard practice for preterm/LBW babies in Indian hospitals.
  • Hold baby upright; rest cup lip on lower lip; let the baby lap/sip — never pour.
  • Easy to clean; no teats to sterilize; supports return to breast.

🍼 Paced bottle (if a bottle is used)

  • Hold baby upright, bottle horizontal; slow-flow newborn teat.
  • Pause every few minutes; let baby draw milk in — don't drip it in.
  • Stop at fullness cues; never prop the bottle or feed a lying-flat baby.

4.8 Working mothers: a sample pumping plan

  • At work: pump every 3 hours (e.g., mid-morning, lunch, mid-afternoon) — 15–20 min double-pumping. Store in a fridge or insulated bag with ice packs.
  • At home: breastfeed directly mornings, evenings, nights, and weekends — direct feeds protect supply better than any pump.
  • Know your rights: Indian law provides maternity leave and workplace nursing breaks — ask your employer/HR (details vary by sector; ASHA/ANM can guide).

4.9 Donor milk & milk banks (brief)

Pasteurized donor human milk from a licensed human milk bank is the recommended fallback when mother's own milk is unavailable (especially for preterm babies — see Ch.6). Donors are screened and milk is heat-treated. Informal milk sharing (unscreened, unpasteurized) carries infection and safety risks and is discouraged.

✅ Key messages — Chapter 4
  1. Hand expression is a core skill — teach every mother before discharge.
  2. Remember 4 hours / 4 days / 6–12 months; label everything.
  3. Thaw slow, warm gentle, swirl + wrist-test — never microwave, never re-freeze.
  4. Cup-feed early EBM where possible; pace any bottle.
  5. Working mothers: pump at work by day, breastfeed directly at home.
👩‍⚕️ For the health workerDemonstrate hand expression on a model or the mother's own breast (with consent) — don't just describe it. Check pump flange size at the first follow-up; wrong size is the commonest cause of "pump doesn't work". Counsel families storing milk for a preterm baby on the stricter NICU rules (see Ch.6).

Chapter 5 — The Term Infant: First Feeds to Six Weeks

The first two weeks set the trajectory: milk supply calibrates, the baby learns to feed, and weight should dip then recover. This chapter gives families a day-by-day map and health workers an assessment flow — so normal variation reassures and real problems surface early.

5.1 The golden first hour

  1. Dry and place skin-to-skin on the mother's chest immediately after birth; cover both with a warm cloth. Delay weighing, bathing, and eye care until after the first feed.
  2. First breastfeed within 1 hour — most vigorous babies self-attach; help with laid-back or cross-cradle hold. Colostrum in teaspoons is exactly the right amount.
  3. No prelacteals — no honey, water, ghutti, or animal milk. (Medicines/vitamins prescribed by the doctor excepted.)
  4. Rooming-in 24 hours — baby stays with the mother day and night; separation only for medical reasons.

5.2 Day-by-day: the first week

Wets: pale, odourless urine. Brick-red "dust" (urates) on day 1–3 alone is common — but WITH poor feeding/sleepiness → get checked.* 💩 Stools: no stool by 48 h, or white/clay stools anytime → doctor. Blood streaks once: often anal fissure — mention at next visit; repeated/bloody + unwell baby → same-day care. ⚖️ Weight: see Fig 5.3 — loss flattens by end of week 1. 💛 Jaundice: yellow eyes/skin spreading downward, poor feeding, or jaundice in the FIRST 24 h → same-day medical review. 😴 Sleepy + not waking to feed → wake, skin-to-skin (§5.5); unwakeable / floppy / cold limbs → emergency. *Urate crystals = concentrated urine; context decides urgency.

5.5 The sleepy baby protocol

  1. Wake every 2–3 hours until birth weight is regained and output is good: unwrap, change nappy, skin-to-skin, stroke feet/back.
  2. Feed at the first stirring — don't wait for full waking or crying.
  3. Use breast compression during the feed (gentle squeeze when sucking slows) to keep milk flowing and baby interested.
  4. Escalate: baby unwakeable for feeds, floppy tone, weak/absent cry, cold hands-feet with pale body, or poor output despite waking → same-day/urgent care (possible infection, hypoglycaemia, jaundice).

5.7 What the term baby does NOT need

  • No water — not even in summer. Breast milk is ~88% water and adjusts to heat via more frequent feeds.
  • No honey (under 1 year — botulism risk), no gripe water, ghutti, or herbal drops.
  • Vitamin D: breastfed infants should receive 400 IU/day vitamin D starting soon after birth (drops, per doctor) — breast milk is naturally low in it.
  • Vitamin K at birth and routine immunizations proceed per hospital/national schedule — they complement, never replace, breastfeeding.

✅ Key messages — Chapter 5
  1. First feed within 1 hour, skin-to-skin, rooming-in, no prelacteals.
  2. Day-matched minimums: wets rise 1→6+, stools black→yellow by day 5.
  3. Enough milk = swallowing + output + weight trend + alert baby together.
  4. 7% loss usual, 10% evaluate, birth weight back by ~2 weeks.
  5. Wake the sleepy newborn q2–3 h; red flags (fever, won't feed, floppy, fast breathing) → same-day care.
👩‍⚕️ For the health workerThe day 3–5 contact is the highest-value visit in this handbook: it catches poor intake before dehydration, engorgement before mastitis, and excessive weight loss before readmission. Always weigh, always watch a feed, always ask about mother's breasts, rest, and mood.
